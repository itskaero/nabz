The content builder

Everything the app offers you — every brand, dose, phrase, chip and advice line — is editable, and none of it is buried in code. This is where you change it, and where a set of deliberate refusals stop a half-finished pack from ever reaching a patient.

What it is for

Nabz ships one content pack: paediatrics. It holds around 150 brands, the doses that carry citations, both locale packs, the examination chips and the investigation list. All of it is data, and this is the editor for it.

Three jobs, in rough order of how often they come up:

  • Make it yours. Add the brands you actually prescribe, rename a chip to the phrase you use, write advice in your own words.
  • Finish what ships unfinished. The seeded rows are honestly marked as unverified. Confirming them is clinical work only a clinician can do.
  • Build a different specialty. The schema is open. A cardiology or ENT pack is a content exercise, not a code change.
The rule the whole thing is built around The library suggests; the prescriber confirms. Nothing here writes a dose, a frequency or a duration onto a script by itself. The builder decides what is offered, never what is entered.

Opening it safely

Settings → Content builder. It opens on a copy, so nothing you do takes effect until you press Save — and while you are in it the prescribing side keeps running on the content it already had.

Save

Publishes the draft. Every script written from that moment uses it. The button stays disabled while anything blocking is outstanding.

Export

Writes the whole pack to a .json file — your backup, and how you move content to another device or hand it to a colleague.

Import

Takes one part of a pack file — just the medicines, just the phrases — and leaves the rest of yours alone. See saving, exporting and importing.

Reset

Throws your edits away and returns to what this build shipped with. It asks first, and it is the one action here you cannot undo. Export before using it.

Content is per device Edits live on the device that made them and do not travel with the queue. A two-machine clinic edits on the doctor's device; the front desk never needs this screen.

Medicines — the catalogue

Brand, generic, strength and form. This is the list that autocompletes when you start typing a drug name.

The generic is the join. A dose is attached to a generic, not to a brand, so every brand of amoxicillin shares one cited dose. That only works if the spelling matches exactly — and the builder refuses to export when it does not, because two spellings of one generic split its dosing in half and neither side is obviously wrong on screen.

Provenance, and why rows say "not verified"

Every shipped row is marked manual and carries no DRAP registration number. That is the honest state of it: a starting vocabulary for the autocomplete, not a checked extract of the national registry.

Reconciling a row means finding it in DRAP's public index, confirming the registration number, and marking it DRAP. The validator will not accept a row that claims DRAP provenance without a number, so the upgrade cannot be done carelessly.

Verify against DRAP yourself, one row at a time Automated matching by brand name is not safe enough to do this for you. Tried on the shipped list, it matched Curam to a different manufacturer's Curamin, plain Panadol to Panadol Night (which adds a second drug), and a tablet to an injection — each time as the only result the registry returned. A wrong registration number is worse than none, because it looks verified.

Importing a list

Import CSV takes a file with at least a brand and a generic column; strength and form are used when present. Everything imported arrives marked manual and unverified — nothing is laundered into looking authoritative by having been in a spreadsheet.

After an import, check the near-duplicate warnings. "Amoxycillin" and "Amoxicillin" are the failure this catches.

Doses — the evidence

A separate table, keyed on generic. Kept apart from the catalogue on purpose: commercial drug listings are useful for names and prices and are not evidence of anything, so their data can never drift into this table.

reference

Required, and enforced. Source, edition and section — for example "WHO Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children, 2nd ed., Annex 2". A row with an empty citation blocks export outright. The citation is both the legal cover and the sanity check the prescriber reads before signing.

verified

Says a clinician has personally checked this row against that source. Seeded rows ship false, and the prescribing screen labels them as unverified — a citation nobody has confirmed must not wear the same authority as one that has been.

A dose whose generic matches no medicine in the catalogue can never be suggested, so that blocks export too. It is almost always a spelling difference. The reverse — a generic with brands but no dose — is only a warning: it means no suggestion appears for that drug, which is a limitation rather than a hazard.

Do not bulk-extract from copyrighted references BNFC, Nelson, Harriet Lane and similar are consult-and-cite: a person reads the source and writes the entry, and the app stores the citation. WHO material is openly licensed and is the exception.

Phrases — how instructions are built

This is where the bilingual output actually comes from. An instruction is not a sentence someone typed; it is a template with slots, filled per language. 

en     {administer} {dose} {frequency}[ {timing}][ for {duration}]
       → Give 5 ml twice a day after food for 7 days

Square brackets mark an optional part: it disappears when the doctor leaves that slot empty. Curly braces are slots filled from the prescription.

Each language is written, never translated The Urdu template is not the English with the words rearranged — it has its own word order and ends where Urdu ends, on the verb. This is the single most important thing about the app, and it is why the builder never offers to fill one language from another.

The tabs also hold vocabulary — the words that fill the slots, like BID → "twice a day" / "دن میں دو بار" — units with their plural forms, and the printed labels for section headings.

Adding a word or a template writes the English and leaves the Urdu blank deliberately. A blank is caught and blocks export; a copy of the English would validate, look finished, and hand a patient a line in a script they cannot read.

The builder also compares slots across languages. If the English has {n} and the Urdu does not, one of them is silently dropping a number the doctor typed — that is an error, not a warning.

Advice and red flags

Two tiers, and they are held to different standards.

Tier 1 — everyday advice

"Complete the full course", "return if the fever lasts more than {'{'}n{'}'} days". One tap on the prescribing screen, both languages pre-written. Add your own with the box at the top of the section; retire one you never use with Retire.

Retiring keeps the wording in the locale packs. A prescription already written referenced that line, and history should still show what the patient was actually handed.

Tier 2 — red flags

"Come back at once if the child is drowsy, not feeding, or struggling to breathe." These are the lines a frightened parent acts on at two in the morning.

Every red flag needs a person's name against it Nothing automatic can tell whether a translation is correct — only that one exists. So a red flag carries the same discipline a dose does: a named human, on a date, saying they read it and it says what it should. Until every one is signed off, the pack will not export.

Type your name in Reviewed by, then confirm each line — or all of them at once when you have read them all. Editing the wording afterwards clears the signature, because a sign-off is on a sentence, not on a name in a list.

Free text is not available in tier 2 on the prescribing screen at all. A red flag can only be chosen from this reviewed list.

Examination chips

Systems, each with the findings offered as tap-chips. The easy tab, because examination is English-only — nobody translates it, so there is no bidi and no plural grammar to get right.

+value

Turns a chip into one that carries a modifier: "hepatomegaly 3cm", "tachypnoea 46/min". The hint you set appears as the placeholder.

Chips are also self-growing: the app notices findings you type by hand repeatedly and offers to promote them, so the palette drifts toward your own practice without anyone maintaining it.

Investigations

Categories — Haematology, Biochemistry, Microbiology, Imaging — each holding the tests offered. Same chip control as examination, and English-only for a blunt reason: a laboratory technician reads "CBC", so a transliterated test name would hand the patient a slip nobody at the lab can act on.

+detail

A qualifier field: "Chest X-ray PA view", "Ultrasound abdomen full bladder".

fasting

Marks a test that needs fasting. It offers the matching advice line when you order that test — it never adds one silently, and the patient-facing wording lives in tier-1 advice where the Urdu has been reviewed.

Nothing here reacts to a diagnosis The palette offers what the specialty commonly orders. Proposing an investigation from a working diagnosis would be clinical decision support, which this product does not do.

Review & export

Everything blocking, and everything worth knowing, in one list. Errors stop the export; warnings do not, but each one is telling you something is less useful than you think.

The tab also carries the pack's authorship — who wrote this content and when. That is not decoration: a pack is a clinical artefact and should say whose judgement is in it.

Why export is blocked

The checks are stricter here than at load time, and deliberately so. When the app loads a pack it degrades as gracefully as it can, because a doctor mid-consultation needs something that works. When you are authoring, a person is present who can fix the problem — so this is the moment to insist.

Blocks exportBecause
A dose with no citation The citation is what makes a suggestion checkable. Without it the app would be asserting a dose on its own authority.
A red flag nobody has signed off Or one whose wording changed after sign-off. Nothing automatic can catch a wrong translation of a return precaution.
A phrase missing or blank in a language The patient gets an empty line, or a line in a script they cannot read.
Languages using different slots One version is dropping a number the doctor typed.
Two spellings of one generic The dosing join splits in half and neither side looks wrong.
A dose for a generic no medicine uses It can never be suggested. Nearly always a spelling difference.
A brand claiming DRAP provenance with no registration number It would look verified without being verifiable.
A sig template offered but not written in a language Choosing it would produce nothing in that language.

Warnings are worth reading too — a generic with brands but no cited dose means no suggestion will ever appear for it, and a category with no tests will only ever be free text.

Saving, exporting and importing

Three different actions that are easy to confuse. They do quite different things, and only one of them is destructive.

Save — makes your edits live

Writes the draft into this device's storage. Every script written from that moment uses it. Nothing leaves the device and no file is produced.

The button stays disabled while anything blocking is outstanding, so a half-finished pack cannot become the one you prescribe from. Content saved this way is per device — it does not travel with the queue, and a two-machine clinic edits on the doctor's device only.

Your work is kept while you edit

The draft is written to this device about a second after you stop typing, so closing the tab or reloading does not lose it. Reopening the builder picks up where you left off and the bar says recovered — it will not silently restore old work and let you mistake it for what the app currently prescribes from.

Saving or Resetting clears it. This is a convenience, not a backup: it lives in the same browser storage as everything else, so Export is still what protects a body of work.

Export — writes a file

One .json file holding the pack and both locale packs, named for the pack and the date. It is plain JSON, not encrypted, and that is deliberate: it contains drug names, chip labels and phrase templates and nothing about any patient. Encrypting it would protect nothing and prevent the one thing it exists for, which is handing it to a colleague.

This is not your records backup Prescriptions, growth charts and history are in the encrypted export under Settings → backups. That one is encrypted, because it holds patient data. A pack file and a records backup are different files for different purposes; do not use one expecting the other.

Export before a Reset, before a risky edit, and whenever you finish a body of work.

You can export unfinished work If the pack still has blocking problems the button reads Export draft, the filename gains -draft, and the file records that it was incomplete. Importing it warns, and its problems reappear as gates on the other side.

Refusing to write a file until a pack is perfect sounds careful and is the opposite — an unfinished pack is the normal state of authoring, and the shipped pack starts with ten unsigned red flags. The gate that matters is Save, which publishes to the prescribing side, and that one stays shut.

Import — takes part of a file

Choose a .json pack and the builder asks which part of it to take. Everything outside that part is left exactly as it was.

Each option says how many rows are coming in and how many of yours it would replace, because this is destructive for the section you pick and there is no undo short of a Reset: 

What should be taken from this file?
colleague-pack.json - everything else in your pack is left alone.

  Medicines          2 coming in  - replaces 150 of yours   [ Take this ]
  Doses             10 coming in  - replaces  10 of yours   [ Take this ]
  Phrases           10 coming in  - replaces  10 of yours   [ Take this ]
  Advice & red flags 24 coming in - replaces  24 of yours   [ Take this ]
  Exam chips        73 coming in  - replaces  73 of yours   [ Take this ]
  Investigations    36 coming in  - replaces  36 of yours   [ Take this ]
  Everything        12 coming in  - replaces 160 of yours   [ Take this ]

The section matching the tab you were on is offered first, since that is almost always what you meant.

What travels with what

Two pairings are deliberate and worth knowing before you rely on them.

Advice

Moves the tier lists, the wording in both languages, and the red-flag sign-offs, together. Splitting them would leave advice ids with no text, or reviewed wording attached to lines that are no longer offered.

Phrases

Moves templates, vocabulary, units and printed labels — but never advice wording. If it did, importing a phrase set would silently replace reviewed return precautions with someone else's words while the sign-off still carried your name.

Imports do not merge row by row. Taking Medicines replaces your catalogue with theirs; it does not add theirs to yours. To combine two lists, export yours, edit the two files together, and import the result — or use Import CSV on the Medicines tab, which does append.

Reset

Throws the draft away and returns to the content this build shipped with. It asks first. It is the only action here you cannot undo, so export before using it.

Moving your setup to a new device

  1. On the old device

    Builder → Export. Keep the file.

  2. On the new device

    Builder → Import → Everything → Save.

  3. Then the records, separately

    Settings → Export my data on the old device, Restore a backup on the new one. Content and records are two different files.

A pack you did not write is clinical content you are adopting Imported red flags arrive with whoever signed them off recorded against them. Read them before prescribing from them; your name goes on the prescription, not theirs.
